## THE BIG STORY · [NEW MODEL]

*[Image: benchmark chart from the lab's announcement. Credit: Lumen Labs]*

### A laptop-sized model now matches last year's best at coding

**Lumen Labs released Ember-8B, an open model that scores 71% on a popular coding test, the same as last year's top closed model.** "8B" means 8 billion parameters, the numbers a model adjusts while it learns. That's small enough to run on a good laptop, with no internet connection.

The lab says the trick was data quality, not size. It trained Ember mostly on carefully filtered code and textbook-style explanations. Independent testers have confirmed the coding score. They also found Ember weaker at long essays and math proofs.

The weights (the trained model files) are free to download, and commercial use is allowed. That means startups, researchers and students can build on it without paying per question.

**Key numbers**
- 71% on the coding test, up from 52% for Lumen's previous model
- About 5 GB to download
- Runs at a usable speed on a laptop with 16 GB of RAM

**Why it matters to you:** You may soon have a decent coding assistant that works offline, costs nothing, and never sends your code to a company's servers. That's useful for projects with private data, and for exam-week Wi-Fi. But "matches on a test" isn't "matches everywhere." Check its fixes like you'd check a classmate's.

## TOOL OF THE WEEK: QuizForge

*[Screenshot: a slide deck turned into 10 practice questions]*

**What it is:** Upload slides or PDF notes and get practice questions with answers.

**Best for:** Reading-heavy courses, such as medicine, business, sciences and engineering theory courses. It's less useful for math-heavy problem sets.

**Try this in 5 minutes:**
1. Upload one lecture's slides.
2. Ask for 10 multiple-choice questions at "exam difficulty."
3. Answer them without looking at the slides.
4. For each wrong answer, open the explanation and check it against your own notes.

**Price:** Free for 5 uploads a month; student plan $4/month.

**Watch out:** It sometimes asks about details your professor never covered, and occasionally marks a wrong answer as right. Treat it as practice, not as the answer key. Check your course policy before uploading a professor's materials.

## WILDCARD · CONCEPT IN 60 SECONDS

### What is an AI "hallucination"?

**A hallucination is when an AI states something false with total confidence, like a citation to a paper that doesn't exist.**

It happens because language models don't look facts up by default. They predict the most likely next words based on patterns in their training data. Most of the time, likely words are also true words. But when the model has seen little about a topic, it still produces something that *sounds* right, because sounding right is what it was trained to do.

Hallucinations are most common with:
- Exact numbers, dates and quotes
- Citations and references
- Niche topics, recent events, and local information

**How to protect yourself:**
- Ask the AI for its sources, then open them. A real link to a real page is the test.
- Use tools that search the web or your own files and show where each claim came from.
- Never paste an AI-generated reference into an assignment without checking it exists.

**So what:** "The AI said so" is not a source. Your professor will check, and so should you.
